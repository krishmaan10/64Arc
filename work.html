The first system we built was our own.
93 branches, on a twenty-year-old ERP nobody wanted to replace.

Partap Singh & Sons distributes commercial-vehicle parts across 93 branches in India. It is a family business, and it is ours — which is why we could try this on a real operation with real money before offering it to anyone else.

PSS · Commercial-vehicle parts distribution · India

A salesman standing in a dealer's shop could not see what that dealer owed.

Before

The business ran — and still runs — on an ERP built in VB.NET two decades ago, living on desktop PCs inside each of 93 branches. Nothing left the building. A rep in a dealer’s shop carried the dues in his head, phoned the branch to ask about stock, and wrote orders on paper. Attendance was a paper register. Whether head office had approved his branch’s credit request, he had no way to see.

Architecture

The ERP stays. It holds twenty years of books and every branch works in it daily — replacing it first would have been the most expensive possible way to start. Instead we put one shared read layer across all 93 branches, and a separate write path for the things the ERP was never able to capture: field activity, orders, attendance, credit decisions.

Platforms

  • Field app for reps — Android and installable web, in 13 languages
  • Dealer portal — each dealer sees their own account and places their own orders
  • Supplier portal — 13 brands see their own stock across the network
  • Head-office desk — credit sanctions, tour and expense approvals
  • ERP rebuild — running beside the original, not yet in its place

Intelligence

Collection lists ordered by what is actually at stake — size, lateness, over limit — rather than by who called last. A dealer overdue at another branch shows up at order time, which a rep could never see before. Orders dictated by voice or photographed off a handwritten slip. A credit check that raises an exception before an order ships, rather than after.

Outcome

The branch PC stopped being the only place the business existed. An order placed in a dealer's shop arrives at the fulfilling branch credit-checked, and opens in the ERP as an ordinary sales order priced by the branch's own masters. A payment cannot be recorded without its proof photograph — they commit together or not at all. And the ERP itself is still the ERP: the rebuild runs in parallel, reading one branch’s real books read-only with every write path unarmed, and until it has earned the switch, it does not get it.

We did not rip out the system the business depends on.
We built around it, and left the books where they were.

Four business shapes, and how we would go about each.

These four are not work we have done. They are common business shapes, written out end to end the way we would approach them, so you can see whether yours is one of them. The only built one on this page is the one above.

Which looks most like your business?

If you are an industrial distributor

A dealer network running on phone calls, spreadsheets and an ERP nobody outside the office can reach.

Before

Dealers order by phone and message. The sales desk re-keys each order, calls the warehouse for stock and accounts for credit. Stock lives in a spreadsheet updated each evening. Nobody can answer "where is my order" without three calls.

Architecture

One definition of product, price, stock and credit at the centre. The ERP keeps operations; everything else reads from a shared layer over it rather than keeping its own copy.

Platforms

  • Dealer ordering app with contract pricing and live credit position
  • Warehouse picking and dispatch interface
  • Sales desk console for exceptions and approvals
  • Management view of channel performance

Intelligence

Replenishment suggestions per dealer from their own buying rhythm. Credit-risk flags before an order is accepted, not after it ships.

What we would expect

The status phone call is the first thing to go. Orders would arrive structured and priced. Stock would be one number everyone shares. The sales desk would move from re-keying to handling the exceptions that actually need judgement.

If you are a manufacturer

Production planned from experience, because no system can see demand and capacity at the same time.

Before

Orders in one system, production tracked on paper and whiteboards, purchasing driven by memory. Shortages are discovered on the line. Planning depends on the two people who have done it for years.

Architecture

Demand, capacity, materials and stock modelled once, so a change in any of them is visible in all of them. Procurement triggered by the plan instead of by recollection.

Platforms

  • Planning board showing demand against capacity
  • Shop-floor application for job status and consumption
  • Procurement workflow driven by material shortfall
  • Quality capture attached to batch and job

Intelligence

Demand forecasting from real order history. Early warning on the items that will run short, with the lead time needed to act on it.

What we would expect

Planning would stop depending on two memories. Shortages would surface weeks earlier instead of at the machine, and what those two people know would be written down where the whole team can use it.

If you sell through more than one channel

An online store that cannot be trusted to know whether an item is actually in stock.

Before

The website holds its own catalogue and its own stock figure, synced overnight. Stores have a third view. Orders are cancelled after being accepted, and the same product is described three different ways.

Architecture

One product record and one live stock position across every channel. Fulfilment decided by rules rather than by whoever notices the order first.

Platforms

  • Storefront reading live availability
  • Store application for stock, transfers and click-and-collect
  • Fulfilment console with routing rules
  • Customer account with orders, returns and history

Intelligence

Recommendations from actual purchase history across channels. Stock rebalancing proposals between locations before something sells out.

What we would expect

Every channel would tell the customer the same thing. Cancellations caused by phantom stock would stop. One catalogue change would update everywhere.

If you run a field service operation

Engineers dispatched by phone, with the customer's history sitting in a filing cabinet.

Before

Complaints arrive by phone and are written down. Engineers are assigned by whoever is free. Spares availability is unknown until the engineer arrives. Contract entitlements are checked by hand, sometimes after the work is done.

Architecture

The installed base, contract terms, service history and spares stock modelled as one thing — so a ticket knows what it is entitled to before anyone travels.

Platforms

  • Customer service-request portal
  • Engineer field application, usable offline
  • Dispatch and scheduling console
  • Spares stock across vans and depots

Intelligence

Tickets flagged before they breach commitment. Likely part identified from symptom and service history so the van is loaded correctly the first time.

What we would expect

Fewer second visits. Entitlement would be known before dispatch, history would travel with the engineer, and the customer could see where their request stands.
