How an engagement runs, from your first email to a branch using the system.

We don’t start with software. The first of five phases maps how your business runs today. The structure is agreed and written down before anything is built, then kept current for as long as the system runs.

Phase 1 of 5

Map the business as it runs.

Before any structure can be decided, the business has to be described as it runs today, including the parts nobody wrote down. A picture like the “before” above is one of the things this phase produces.

What gets mapped

The map covers people, roles and processes as they are performed, which often differs from the org chart, and the customers, dealers and partners around them. It lists each system in use, what it is used for, and where information gets carried from one to another by hand.

Where the mapping happens

On the floor and in the field as well as in meeting rooms, including the spreadsheets and message threads people keep beside the ERP. Those hold the steps the ERP leaves out, and a workaround of that kind usually turns out to be a requirement.

What comes out

The phase ends with a process map, a systems inventory, an information-flow diagram and a ranked list of bottlenecks, each traced to a cost the business is paying.

Phase 2 of 5

Decide the structure.

This phase decides what stays, what changes, what connects, what gets built and where AI belongs. The rest of the engagement depends on it, so the structure is agreed with the people who run the company before it is built, and it is written so that they can explain it back.

The business model

Customer, product, price, order and stock position each get one definition, agreed across departments that define them differently today.

The platform map

It lists every screen, who uses it, and what each role is allowed to see.

Keep, replace or build

Each existing system is marked keep, replace or build, and the reasoning is written down so the decision can be read later instead of argued again.

Where AI belongs

The decisions where rules or a model, given the right records, would change the outcome are listed. Where AI would only be decoration, it stays off the list.

Phase 3 of 5

Build what the structure calls for.

The work is built in sequence and released in pieces a branch can use. Every piece sits on the same foundation, so there are no separate products to join up later.

Foundation first

The shared business model, the data layer, permissions and the rules are built before the screens that depend on them, so the credit check on a dealer’s order runs on the server whether a rep or the dealer places it.

Released in working slices

Each release is something one team can start using, so nothing waits for a single launch date at the end.

Built with your people

The team that will use a screen works with it while it is being made, and their changes go in before it is released.

Designed for all-day use

Screens that staff use for a full shift get the same design care as the ones customers see.

Nothing has to be migrated

Your books stay where they are. The new system reads from the software that keeps them and sends orders and credit decisions back in a form it already reads, so the ERP still does the posting. A replacement runs beside the original until you have watched it work.

Documented as built

The structure, the data model and the integrations are written down as they are built, and each capability carries a status, from built to not built yet.

Phase 4 of 5

Make it one system.

Screens, data and workflows are joined until information moves on its own, including through the systems you chose to keep. This phase is what turns the “before” at the top of the page into the “after”.

Systems you keep

Accounting, payroll, statutory tools and specialist machinery software stay where they are, and the new system connects to them.

Outside services

Each outside service is connected once, on the server, and every screen uses that connection. Order mail to the branches and a GSTIN check when a dealer is added work this way. Payment gateways, logistics and marketplace connections are not built yet.

How an order crosses the line

In the system 64ARCS built for PSS, a parts distributor, an order leaves the app with no prices on it. The branch clerk opens the file from the order mail in the ERP’s own sale-order screen, and the ERP prices it from the branch’s masters. The file is named after the order, so the ERP refuses to save the same order twice. The ERP stays the system of record. How that was built

Manual steps that go away

The re-keying, the reconciliation, the status phone call and the daily spreadsheet export stop being anyone’s job, and the people who did them move to work that needs judgement.

The connection counts as finished only when nobody carries a number from one screen to another by hand.

Phase 5 of 5

Keep extending it.

The system is not handed over and left. 64ARCS stays on, and new capability attaches to the structure that already holds your orders, stock and dues.

New interfaces

A new region, channel, brand or partner type gets its own screen without a new data silo behind it.

New intelligence

As the system builds up its own history, more decisions become worth automating or forecasting. Demand forecasting, for one, is not built yet.

Structure review

The structure is reviewed as the business changes, and the written version is updated with it.

Support that knows the system

The people who maintain it are the people who designed it. Nothing is switched off until you have seen its replacement run.

What 64ARCS will not do.

01

No rip-and-replace mandate

Systems that still serve the business stay, and are connected instead of discarded.

02

No one-size-fits-all ERP

The structure follows how your business works. Your business is not asked to reshape itself around a product roadmap.

03

No AI for the sake of AI

AI is used only where it changes a decision.
